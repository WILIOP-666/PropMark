## The question every buyer asks
You find a home you like. The agent quotes a price. A property portal shows a wide range. How do you know if that price is fair, or if you are about to overpay by a hundred thousand dollars?
The usual answer is to look at recent sales of similar homes nearby. The catch is that most tools match homes on little more than the number of bedrooms and bathrooms. That puts a freshly renovated home next to a tired one on the same street and calls them the same. They are not.
[See why bedroom-and-bathroom comparisons fall short →](https://www.microburbs.com.au/cma/whitepaper#problem)
## What a real comparison looks like
This is a comparison, not just a single number. We line a home up against genuinely similar recent sales, check that they really do look alike, and show which features match and which explain the price difference. Our comparison reads dozens of things about a home, the pool, the views, the renovation, the garage, the aspect, the street, not just how many bedrooms it has.
Here is how that works, shown for two real Penrith homes a buyer would weigh against each other. The features pulled into the middle are shared, so the homes are genuinely comparable. The ones on the right are what lifts the dearer home's price.

## What we built
We matched homes right across the country to recent nearby sales of genuinely similar homes, using far more than bedrooms and bathrooms. Then we adjusted each past sale for how the market has moved since, so the figure reflects today, not two years ago.
It covers all eight states and territories. For about **9 in 10 of the homes we cover** (roughly 85% of all homes), there are enough genuine nearby sales to form an estimate. We focus on normal homes, and deliberately leave out the largest blocks, about **1 in 13 homes** (farms, acreage and big rural land), because a 50 hectare farm is not a fair comparison for a suburban house. Those are marked out of scope rather than given a number.

## And the number it produces lands close too
The comparison above is the heart of it. The single dollar figure it rolls up to is just the summary, and here is how close that summary lands. **For a typical home it is within about 10 to 20% of the real sale price.** On an $800,000 home, that means a typical estimate sits somewhere around $680,000 to $880,000. Close enough to tell whether an asking price is fair, not a formal valuation.
It is strongest in the mid-market, where most homes trade. Below, the estimate for a typical house is plotted across the price range, from an Adelaide starter suburb to blue-chip Sydney, against what houses there have actually sold for. Dots near the line are estimates that matched the market.

The same holds across the country: for a typical home the estimate lands within about 10 to 20% of the eventual sale price. It is most reliable in the mid-market where most homes trade, and a little rougher right at the top end and in quiet markets where few similar homes change hands. Treat it as a strong starting point and a sanity check, not a formal valuation.
**Every estimate tells you how much to trust it.** It is strongest where plenty of similar homes have sold close by, weaker where few have, and where there is not enough to go on we hold the number back rather than guess. Each estimate is dated, so you always know it reflects the market now.
## How this helps you
Before you offer, you can check the asking price against what comparable homes nearby actually sold for. If the quote sits well above genuinely similar sales, that is a reason to pause, or to negotiate.
It is not a growth forecast. Its value is making sure you do not overpay. Buying in line with, or below, what comparable homes sold for is instant equity, and it protects the return on your purchase.
