# The Power of Leverage: Why a 20% Deposit Is Property's Secret Weapon (And Its Biggest Risk)
![David Bailey](https://www.microburbs.com.au/images/david-bailey.png)
David BaileyMarch 20, 2026

Ask any seasoned property investor what gives real estate its edge over shares, and most will give you the same answer: **leverage**. It is the financial equivalent of a force multiplier — the ability to control a large asset with a relatively small amount of your own capital. And in Australia, where banks will happily lend you 80% of a property's value, leverage is arguably the single most powerful wealth-building mechanism available to ordinary people.
But leverage is not a free lunch. It is a double-edged sword that amplifies everything — gains _and_ losses. Understanding both sides of this equation is what separates sophisticated investors from speculators who get wiped out.
## The Math: How Leverage Supercharges Returns
Let us run the numbers on a straightforward example. You purchase a $500,000 property with a 20% deposit — $100,000 of your own money — and borrow $400,000 from the bank.
Australian property has historically grown at around **6.8% to 7.25% per year** over the past 30 years, according to Reserve Bank of Australia data. Let us use 7% for simplicity.
  * **Year 1 capital growth:** $500,000 x 7% = $35,000
  * **Your equity invested:** $100,000
  * **Return on YOUR money:** $35,000 / $100,000 = **35%**


Read that again. The property grew 7%, but your return on equity was **35%**. That is the leverage multiplier at work — a 5:1 ratio turning modest asset growth into extraordinary returns on invested capital.
Now compare that with an unleveraged share portfolio. The S&P/ASX 200 has delivered roughly **9-10% per year** including dividends and franking credits over the long term. Invest $100,000 in shares and you earn approximately $10,000 in year one. A solid return — but less than a third of what the leveraged property investor earned on the same capital.
> This is not an argument that property is "better" than shares. It is an argument that leverage fundamentally changes the return equation — and most comparisons between the two asset classes fail to account for it.
## Why Banks Love Property (And Why That Matters)
Here is the crucial detail most people overlook: the reason property leverage works so well is that **banks treat property as uniquely safe collateral**. You can borrow 80% of a property's value at mortgage rates (currently around 6%), but try borrowing 80% to buy shares and you will either be refused or charged punishing margin loan rates.
APRA data confirms this conservatism works. Less than 10% of new Australian home loans are written at LVRs above 90%, compared to nearly 50% in countries that experienced housing busts during the GFC. Australian lending standards, enforced through APRA's macroprudential toolkit — including caps on interest-only lending, investor loan growth limits, and the new debt-to-income ratio ceiling of 6x introduced in February 2026 — create guardrails that most other countries lack.
This regulatory framework is precisely what makes Australian property leverage relatively safe. But "relatively" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence.
## The Dark Side: When Leverage Destroys Wealth
Leverage does not care which direction prices move. If a $500,000 property falls 10% to $450,000, you have lost $50,000 — that is **50% of your $100,000 deposit gone**. A 20% decline wipes out your entire equity. And unlike shares, you cannot sell a bedroom to cut your losses.
This is not theoretical. It happened. Twice, in recent Australian history.
**Perth, 2014-2019:** When the mining boom unwound, Western Australian property values fell by **16.1%**. The median house price dropped from $560,000 to $480,000 between 2015 and 2017. Mining towns like Karratha and Port Hedland lost up to **40% of their value**. Investors who had bought at the peak with 20% deposits found themselves deep in negative equity — owing the bank more than their property was worth, with no way out except to sell at a loss or hold on and keep paying.
**Sydney, 2017-2019:** After APRA tightened lending rules, Sydney property fell **14-16% from its mid-2017 peak**. The value of investor lending in NSW collapsed by almost 50%. The RBA estimated that approximately **400,000 Australian homeowners** were in negative equity during this period. For recent buyers who had stretched to buy with minimal deposits, the leverage that was supposed to build wealth was now destroying it.
> The RBA's own research paper "How Risky is Australian Household Debt?" found that highly leveraged borrowers — those with high LVR or high loan-to-income ratios — were the most likely to fall into arrears. Higher leverage did not just amplify losses; it actively increased the probability of financial distress.
## Interest Rates: The Hidden Leverage Risk
There is another dimension to leverage risk that gets less attention: **the cost of carrying the debt**. Unlike shares, where your unleveraged position has no ongoing cost, a leveraged property requires you to service a mortgage — every single month, regardless of what the market does.
When the RBA raised the cash rate from 0.1% to 4.35% between 2022 and 2023, monthly repayments on a $400,000 loan jumped by roughly $1,000 per month. For investors relying on rental income to cover costs, this was the property equivalent of a margin call — except you cannot simply sell and walk away without potentially crystallising a massive loss.
This is the leverage trap: **you are locked in**. Unlike a share portfolio you can liquidate in minutes, selling a property takes months, costs tens of thousands in agent fees and stamp duty (already paid), and may require you to sell at the worst possible time.
## The Smart Approach to Leverage
None of this means leverage is bad. It means leverage demands **respect**. The investors who build wealth through property leverage tend to follow a few principles:
  * **Buy in markets with structural demand** — population growth, infrastructure investment, supply constraints. Mining towns and speculative fringe developments are leverage graveyards.
  * **Stress-test your serviceability** — can you afford repayments if rates rise 2-3%? If rental vacancy hits 8 weeks? If both happen simultaneously?
  * **Maintain a cash buffer** — the RBA's financial stability research consistently shows that borrowers with savings buffers are dramatically less likely to default, even in downturns.
  * **Think in decades, not years** — leverage rewards patience. Perth's market has since recovered and surpassed its previous peaks. Sydney's 2017-2019 dip is barely visible on a 20-year chart. But you have to survive the dip to benefit from the recovery.
  * **Do not confuse leverage with skill** — in a rising market, every leveraged buyer looks like a genius. The real test is whether your investment thesis holds when the tide goes out.


## The Bottom Line
Leverage is property's superpower. A 20% deposit giving you 100% exposure to an appreciating asset is genuinely extraordinary — no other mainstream investment vehicle offers this combination of accessibility, scale, and bank willingness to lend. Over the long run, the mathematics of leveraged property returns are compelling.
But leverage is not magic. It is a tool. Used wisely, with the right asset, adequate buffers, and a long time horizon, it can genuinely transform household wealth. Used recklessly — in the wrong market, with too little margin for error — it can be financially devastating.
The smartest property investors are not the ones who use the most leverage. They are the ones who understand exactly what leverage is doing to their risk profile and plan accordingly.
